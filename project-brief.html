Who Lived When

A history app you step into: one place, a few years, and the people who lived it, across social classes

Brief updated 3 October 2026, after the first version was built

1 · The idea

Standing at a point in time and seeing everyone who was there: the king, the composer, the novelist, and the farmhand who never left his village.

Zola's "J'accuse…!" ran in L'Aurore in 1898. Clemenceau gave it its headline.
Cézanne and Zola met as schoolboys in Aix, and stayed entangled for forty years.
A Loire Valley farmhand worked land he didn't own while Impressionism was taking shape in Paris salons.

The app opens on moments (one place over a few years), tells each one as a short guided story over a painting of the period, and grounds every relationship in something that actually happened rather than a vague label.

Scope of the first version

  • Four places (France, Japan, Britain, China), ten moments, four written stories: Paris 1870s, Paris 1890s, Tokyo 1870s, Kyoto 1590s
  • 96 people, 23 everyday lives, 43 dated events, 59 eras
  • English
  • A personal project, and a Spring Boot and Kotlin portfolio piece

Two rules that shape everything

Facts are sourced; prose is drafted from sources and reviewed. Dates, places and events are curated data (a Wikidata import replaces the hand-typed seed later). Bios and portraits come from Wikipedia. Story text is drafted by Claude from sourced Wikipedia text, with a quote behind every line, and a person reviews it before it ships.
Every relationship is anchored to a dated, sourced event. Not "they were friends" but "he gave the headline to the letter, 1898." A claim that can't be pinned to something documented doesn't ship.

2 · Design decisions

We tried three ways into history before settling. The finished direction is the third.

DroppedThe year dial. Pick a place, drag a slider across 5,000 years, see who is alive with their age. It explained the data well but felt like a control panel: place picker, slider, era band and chips all at once, with no immersion.
DroppedThe long gallery. A 3D floor of time you pinch, drag and fly through (the childhood idea of a room-sized timeline). Fun, but "hard to browse as a human": time, place, zoom, depth and people to steer at once, and no path through it.
KeptStories are the way in, and browsing happens between stories. A moment is a place over roughly 10 to 30 years, small enough to tell as one story. A story is 6 to 9 cards over a painting: setting the scene, someone who lived it, an everyday life, documented events. It ends with doors to other moments: Meanwhile, elsewhere (Paris 1870s to Tokyo 1870s), Later, same place, or Follow someone (Zola and Clemenceau into Paris 1890s). You move through history room by room, like a museum.
KeptThe world around. Every moment has four lines (who governs, everyday life, arts and ideas, meanwhile elsewhere), plus live chips of who is alive in other places in the year you are looking at. A person's page shows the world around them too.
KeptTheir life in their time. A person's timeline of regime changes and documented events, with their age at each ("30: the Third Republic begins"). It is computed from dated records, so it is accurate by construction. Ages skip the year 0, which never existed.
KeptPlaces through today's borders, eras as who governed. A place is France or Japan; its eras are the regimes over time (Third Republic, Edo period). Consecutive eras share their boundary year, and the later one wins it.
KeptEveryday lives are always there. Typical lives (a washerwoman on the Seine, a worker at the Tomioka silk mill) sit next to rulers and artists, shown as illustrated and representative of the period, never as real people.
KeptPeriod artwork and a dark gallery look. Public-domain paintings from Wikimedia Commons fill the screen, credited and linked; a story card can bring its own (Hiroshige III's steam train for Tokyo's first railway). The theme is a warm near-black with one lamp-gold accent, Newsreader for story text, Geist for the interface, condensed numerals for years.
KeptThree layers of content. Facts are curated data. Computed views (ages, who was alive together, life in their time, suggested doors) are code. Prose (story cards, world lines, hooks) is drafted by Claude once per moment and reviewed. Nothing is generated live for visitors.

Mockups from these explorations are private artifacts on claude.ai: Who Lived When Timeline Studies (claude.ai/artifact/WJeRTuF4D7zWQuZYn7qe3c), The Long Gallery (claude.ai/artifact/KdedR72zL5NRAMsPZhvCQt) and Doors Through Time (claude.ai/artifact/JQHGcdqABjrnofVfEcue5s), the one the app follows.

Anatomy of a story card

PaintingThe moment's artwork behind every card, slowly zooming; a card can replace it with its own. Credit and source link always visible.
ProgressOne bar per card plus the doors, always visible, so the format never implies more content than exists.
Kicker"Setting the scene", "Someone who lived it", "Everyday life", or "Documented event · 1874".
Role chipsEvent cards pair each person with their role ("author", "chose the headline") and link to their page.
Source lineEvent cards always show their source.
DoorsThe last card: two or three curated ways out, and a link to meet everyone in the moment.

5 · Structure and data

Entities. Region (a place) · Era (a region over a span, named after who governed) · Person (with Wikipedia lead, link and portrait) · Life (a typical existence in an era) · Event and EventParticipant (dated, sourced, with roles) · Moment (place, period, focus year, hook, Artwork, WorldAround, status DRAFT or PUBLISHED, featured) · StoryCard (SCENE, PERSON, LIFE or EVENT, in order, optional own artwork) · Door (curated way to another moment, with faces).
Seed data. backend/src/main/resources/seed/: regions, eras, people, lives, events, moments (with world lines, story cards and doors). Loaded by SeedData; WikipediaJob then fills bios and portraits in the background.
API. GET /api/moments · /api/moments/{id} · /api/moments/{id}/story · /api/people/{slug}?year= · /api/years/{year}/people?exclude=FR · /api/search?q= · /api/regions · /api/eras · /api/eras/{id}
Drafting a story. ./gradlew bootRun --args='--app.drafting.moment=edo-1830s --server.port=0' with ANTHROPIC_API_KEY set. Writes backend/drafts/<moment>.json: the draft, its sources and the validator's findings, for a curator to correct and copy into seed/moments.json. Not yet run against the API in this project (no key was available); the dry run reaches the API.

6 · Handover: deploying to the VPS

For the next session. Goal: the app reachable on a public HTTPS address from the VPS, so a friend can try it.

Where things stand

  • Repo: a single git repository at the project root on branch main, nothing committed yet, no remote. Commit and push first, then clone on the VPS.
  • Runs locally as: backend cd backend && ./gradlew bootRun on port 8080 (Java 25); frontend cd frontend && npm ci && npm run build && npm start on port 3000 (Node 24).
  • One public port: the browser only talks to the frontend. frontend/next.config.ts rewrites /api/* to BACKEND_URL (server-side env, default http://localhost:8080). Keep the backend private.
  • No database to run: H2 in memory with create-drop. Each start reseeds from the JSON files and refetches Wikipedia bios and portraits in the background (about a minute). No volume needed.
  • Images: the browser loads paintings and portraits straight from upload.wikimedia.org and thumb.wikimedia.org (next/image unoptimized), so nothing to host.
  • Not needed to deploy: ANTHROPIC_API_KEY. It is only for the drafting job, run by hand.

Suggested setup: Docker Compose and Caddy

  1. Backend image. Multi-stage: build with eclipse-temurin:25-jdk and ./gradlew bootJar; run the jar on eclipse-temurin:25-jre. Set JAVA_TOOL_OPTIONS=-XX:MaxRAMPercentage=75.
  2. Production config. Add application-prod.yaml (activated with SPRING_PROFILES_ACTIVE=prod) with spring.h2.console.enabled: false: the H2 console must never be reachable publicly. Put a contact (repo URL or email) in app.wikipedia.user-agent, as Wikimedia asks.
  3. Frontend image. Add output: "standalone" to next.config.ts; build on node:24-alpine, run node server.js on port 3000. Gotcha: rewrites are resolved at build time, so pass BACKEND_URL=http://backend:8080 as a build argument as well as a runtime environment variable (server components read it at runtime).
  4. Compose. Three services: backend (no published port), frontend (depends on backend), caddy (ports 80 and 443, reverse_proxy frontend:3000), all restart: unless-stopped.
  5. Address. The user's domain, or a free <ip>.sslip.io hostname; Caddy gets the HTTPS certificate on its own. Open only ports 80 and 443 on the VPS firewall.
  6. Size. About 1 GB of RAM is enough (JVM 300 to 500 MB, Node about 150 MB); 2 GB is comfortable. Both base images exist for ARM and x86.
  7. Deploy and update. git clone then docker compose up -d --build; later git pull && docker compose up -d --build. docker compose logs -f backend should show "Enriched N of N people from Wikipedia".
  8. Smoke test on the public address. /, /moment/paris-1870s/story, /person/emile-zola?year=1875, /api/search?q=zola all load; /h2-console returns 404.

Ask the user first

  • VPS operating system, architecture and RAM; whether Docker is installed
  • Domain name, or use sslip.io
  • Where the code lives (GitHub remote?) and whether to commit now

Open
